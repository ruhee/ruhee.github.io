July was such a packed month—I'm exhausted but for entirely great reasons.

This month I achieved a really exciting personal milestone—after 20 years of living in Toronto, I finally played a show at the [Rex Hotel](https://www.therex.ca/), the legendary jazz bar on Queen St.

When I moved here for school in 2006 (oof), I was studying jazz and dreamed of playing at the Rex one day. Instead, I ended up playing in indie rock bands and never really played much jazz at all outside of school, and so I assumed this one-time dream would never happen. But the great [Friendly Rich](https://friendlyrich.com/) asked me to sub in with his Lollipop People at a late-night Rex set last month, and I did, and it was so awesome. I had a great time and I think I was even tricked into playing some actual jazz... I feel very grateful to know so many cool musicians who want me to make art with them!

I also read a few books I really liked this month. One of them was [_The Clarion_](https://invisiblepublishing.com/product/the-clarion/) by Nina Dunic, a beautiful and melancholy piece of literary fiction with some nice music-related details. I loved the writing style—it's so spare but so _full_ somehow, like each word was absolutely necessary and filled with meaning. It's one of those books where not a lot of plot happens, but so much develops inside the characters' heads and lives, and you can really feel the humanity in there. I loved it, highly recommend.

Another was Patrick Radden Keefe's [_London Falling_](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/704979/london-falling-by-patrick-radden-keefe/9780385675482) which I mentioned a few months ago after reading his original New Yorker article about this story and learning that I'd read it _the day before_ this book came out, lol. I listened to the audiobook, which Keefe read himself. The book sprawls out into all kinds of history—including _my_ ancestral family history, about the migration of workers from India to East Africa and then Idi Amin's expulsion of Asians from the region! I sure wasn't expecting that—but what felt like digressions eventually tied themselves back to the main idea, which (to me) was that every person in the world is affected by myriad factors that influence how they think and act, and we can't always know what motivates them. This book was a strange true-crime investigation and historical document and third-person memoir in a way. I will read anything Patrick Radden Keefe writes, and I thought the audiobook was a great way to experience this one.
